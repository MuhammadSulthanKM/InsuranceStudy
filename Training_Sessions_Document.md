### Key Concepts and Definitions
- **Insurance** - Mechanism to make good the losses suffered by the insured when a covered incident occurs.
- **Insurer / Insured** - Insurer is the company (e.g. Tata AIA, ICICI Prudential, HDFC Life, SBI Life); insured is the party buying cover. (Transcript mixes the two words; corrected from context.)
- **Premium** - Payment by the insured (one-time, monthly or annual) to keep the policy in force; without exchange of premium there is no enforceable contract.
- **Policy / contract** - Agreement between insurer and insured defining coverage, terms and conditions.
- **Pooling of premium** - Premiums collected from many individuals, from which claims of the affected are paid.
- **Probability / law of large numbers** - Mathematical concepts used with historical data to predict expected losses and set rates.
- **Underwriting** - Process of assessing risk, deciding whether to insure and how much premium to charge.
- **Claim** - Request for payment after a covered loss.
- **Deductible (excess, franchise, co-pay)** - Amount borne by the insured before the insurer pays; discourages small claims and lowers premium when higher. Co-pay is the health insurance term, e.g. 10% of claim.
- **Super top-up** - Additional health policy triggered only once the base policy sum insured is exhausted (e.g. 5 lakh base plus 95 lakh top-up).
- **Policy limit** - Maximum the insurer pays for a covered loss; equal to sum insured.
- **Insurable interest** - Damage to the object must cause the insured a financial loss; you cannot insure the Taj Mahal.
- **Property insurance** - Covers damage to physical property (car, home, building, belongings) from fire, theft, windstorm, vandalism.
- **Casualty** - Legal liability for injury or damage; the bigger umbrella under which liability insurance (product, general, D&O) falls.
- **Liability** - Responsibility to pay for damage or injury caused to a third party.
- **Product liability** - Manufacturer pays those harmed by a product (e.g. hazardous ingredient, contaminated cough syrup).
- **D&O (Directors and Officers) liability** - Covers key management who may be personally sued, even where the business pays.
- **Workers' compensation** - Employer's liability for injury/disability of an employee during duty.
- **Umbrella / excess policy** - Additional liability limits above underlying home and auto policies.
- **Producer / agent / broker** - Intermediaries who sell policies on behalf of the insurer.
- **Claim adjuster** - Sets the claim, decides amount payable and whether to accept or deny.
- **Reinsurance** - Insurance for insurers; mentioned but skipped for time.
- **Dwelling** - Home structure: walls, roof, foundation.
- **Peril** - Specific cause of loss. **Named peril** covers only listed perils; **open peril (all-risk)** covers everything except listed exclusions.
- **Rider** - Extra cover bought by paying additional premium (e.g. flood, earthquake).
- **Endorsement (amendment)** - Change made to an existing policy after issuance (contact address, deductible).
- **Replacement cost** - Cost to replace with a similar item restoring the same condition.
- **Condominium** - North American term for flats/units.

### Detailed Notes
#### How insurance works
Insurer and insured enter a legally enforceable contract (in India stamp duty is paid and it is registered). The insured pays premium on a one-time, monthly or annual schedule; the insurer promises compensation under the contract terms. Because losses cannot be predicted individually, the insurer acts as an aggregator, pooling risk from many people. Using probability and the law of large numbers with historical data, it builds rate tables. Example: a neighbourhood of 1,000 homes worth $300,000 each; statistically 2 to 3 homes suffer a serious fire loss averaging $150,000 per year, so each homeowner contributes about $300 to $400 premium per year. The principle is common to life, P&C and health.

#### Underwriting factors
- Life: age, occupation (e.g. oil work vs. desk job), adverse habits (smoking, drinking), health history such as hereditary diabetes.
- Home: location (flood plain vs higher ground; bushfire areas like California or Australia; tornado areas in the US, Canada, Australia), neighbourhood theft history (home policies also cover goods such as TVs, furniture, jewellery).
- Motor: driving record, e.g. DUIs (driving under influence) or prior accidents or traffic violations lead to higher charges. Anything raising the chance of loss is an underwriting parameter.

#### Deductibles and policy structure
Deductibles discourage small claims because paperwork and management cost is similar for a $1,000 and a $10,000 claim. Higher deductible gives lower premium; Indian zero-dep motor policy example had a 1,000 rupee deductible, customisable to 10,000 with a reduced premium. Super top-up health plans are triggered only when the underlying policy is exhausted and may come from a different insurer; wording determines what is covered.

#### Multiple policies and indemnity
In P&C and health, the insurer pays only up to the actual loss. If two 5 lakh policies exist and the loss is 7 lakh, you claim 5 lakh from one and the remaining 2 lakh from the other; you cannot collect the full amount from each. Life insurance differs: multiple policies on one life each pay the full sum insured. Policy wording matters (e.g. maternity claims may exclude surgical gloves, ICU or physiotherapy items).

#### Types of insurance and P&C lines
Three categories: life, health, and property and casualty (called general insurance in India). Major P&C lines listed: homeowners (HO, owner-occupied dwelling, belongings, liability); renters (belongings and liability although the structure is not owned); personal auto (variations: commercial auto such as trucks, ride-sharing like Uber or Ola); commercial property (buildings, equipment, inventory, factories, boilers); general liability (third-party injury or property damage); workers' compensation (e.g. warehouse worker hurts back lifting a load); umbrella/excess liability.
Auto policies have own damage and third-party parts; the attendee's assumption that own damage maps to property and third party to casualty was confirmed. In India third-party liability is mandatory; in the US you can be sued for anything (e.g. a visitor slipping on a wet floor), so business owners buy liability policies.

#### Regulation
India: IRDAI, policies must be submitted before marketing. US: insurance is a state subject, so a New York policy may differ from a Florida policy from the same company.

#### Home insurance forms
- **HO1** - Basic named-peril form: fire and lightning, windstorm and hail, explosion, riot and civil commotion, damage by aircraft, vehicles, smoke, vandalism, theft, volcanic eruption.
- **HO2** - Broad form, named peril, adding perils such as falling objects, weight of ice/snow/sleet (roofs of wooden houses can collapse), water or steam damage from plumbing/AC/automatic fire systems, accidental tearing apart/cracking/burning, frozen pipes, power surges. Dwelling at replacement cost; personal belongings at actual cash value.
- **HO3** - Special form (open peril): covers all damage except flood, earthquake, war, nuclear accident, landslide, mudslide, sinkhole. These exclusions can be added via riders for extra premium.
- **HO4** - Renters: belongings and liability, not the building.
- **HO5** - Comprehensive: wider cover for belongings including jewellery, furs and electronics; trainer noted dwelling at replacement cost and that premium rises with coverage.
- **Condo form** - For units in a shared building; covers interior unit, belongings, liability; common areas, landscaping and external walls are the association's (society's) responsibility and are insured separately.
- **Mobile homes** - Trailers and RVs; uncommon.
- **Modified coverage** - For older homes where replacement cost exceeds market value.
Insurers generally exclude terrorism and flood by default, available for extra premium but depends on the company; if a company does not offer a cover (e.g. own damage for vehicles over 10 years in India), look for another insurer.

#### Homeowners coverage sections
Dwelling (roof, foundation); other structures (garage, shed, fence); personal property (furniture, electronics, clothing); loss of use / additional living expenses (hotel stay after a fire); personal liability (legal defence costs and judgments if someone is injured at the premises); medical payments to others (small medical bills for an injured guest regardless of fault). Summarised as A, B, C, D, E, F.

#### Replacement cost
Explained via a 10-year-old discontinued car that is totalled: repair may be costly or parts unavailable, so the insurer may replace with a similar vehicle of similar value, restoring the same condition.

After access is granted, he will show end-to-end flow: create account, create policy, and policy transactions such as renewal, rewrite, cancellation, reinsurance (as transcribed).

#### Insurance in the US
Premium modes include monthly, quarterly, semi-annual, annual; Guidewire's billing screens also offer these. Home insurance is described as effectively mandatory in the US and rental insurance is mandatory (a rental cannot be issued without it). Reason for home cover: snowstorms, volcanoes, hurricanes and the fact that about 80% of houses in cold coastal areas are built of wood. USAA site shows vehicle insurance (auto, motorcycle, ATV, RV, boat, classic car, aviation), property (home, condo, landlord, personal property, flood, mostly for seacoast residents), life (term, whole, final expense, children's whole life) and Medicare health plans.

#### Personal and commercial lines
Personal auto and home are personal lines. Home sub-products: HO series and Dwelling Fire (DP1, DP2, DP3). Commercial lines: four main product lines (commercial auto, CPP, workers' comp, BOP/umbrella) each with sub-LOBs; USAA reportedly does not support commercial clients. Team works only on auto, so no deep dive on other lines.

#### Getting a quote and agent role
Guidewire is internal, so customers cannot log in. A customer enters name, date of birth, address and vehicle details on the website to get a quote; the zip code (e.g. 61704, Bloomington, Illinois) sends an alert to authorised local agents, creating a lead/activity in Guidewire. Alternatively, the customer calls an agent who creates the account. The customer cannot complete the application alone; at some point the agent must interact and complete the flow.

#### Underwriting and pricing
Premium depends on credit score, incidents, driving history and speeding tickets. A poor credit score may mean a declined quote, and even rentals require credit checks. Other pricing factors: value of vehicle (new 2026 model costs more), age and pre-existing conditions for life/health, location near coast, flood or hurricane areas. Underwriters review risky applications (many incidents/tickets/high value): the agent cannot approve and routes it to underwriting; the underwriter may request evidence and then approves or rejects with reasons. In day-to-day project work the tester submits as an agent and then logs in as an underwriter to approve or issue.

#### Claims
A reported incident creates a claim assigned to an adjuster who investigates, assesses damage and cost, and processes it after the deductible. Claim Center holds claims only; policy details are fetched from Policy Center; the three centres are integrated.

#### State rules and licences
Each state has its own laws, rules and licence formats; moving permanently (e.g. New York to Boston) requires a new state licence test, unlike India's nationwide licence.

#### Policy structure and drivers
Declarations show insured, limits, term and premium. Key US difference: in India the car is insured whoever drives; in the US the driver must be listed on the policy. Spouse, parent or friend not listed will not have a claim approved. Up to 6 drivers and 6 vehicles per policy (trainer's recollection, believed common across providers and states, to be verified in Guidewire). Each driver needs name, date of birth, date of first licence, incidents and tickets; these drive the premium. A driver need not own a car (roommates can both be listed). Exclusions: unlisted drivers. Endorsement: adding a driver mid-term, similar to a rider in India.

#### Pooling example
100 cars each paying a premium (e.g. 10,000); only about 10% to 20% have claims in a year, so the pool pays them.

### Key Concepts and Definitions
- **Coverage limit** - The sum insured; the amount payable in an event of total loss, based on what is declared.
- **Deductible** - Minimum amount deducted from any loss; higher deductible means lower premium.
- **Co-pay** - Share of claim borne by the insured (e.g. 20% on parents' health policy; up to 50% on OPD visits in the trainer's example).
- **Protective devices** - Fire sprinklers, extinguishers, alarm systems; lower the risk and premium.
- **Credit-based insurance score** - Used as a rating factor; low scores or defaults may raise moral hazard of intentional damage.
- **Depreciation** - Reduction in value over time (the trainer mentions 5% to 10% per year as a typical assumption).
- **Zero depreciation** - A rider (India motor) paying without depreciation deduction; another rider returns invoice value.
- **IDV / sum insured** - Insured declared value for own damage.
- **Indemnity** - Insurer pays up to the actual damages, not necessarily the full sum insured.
- **Own damage vs third party** - Own damage is affected by depreciation/IDV; third-party premium is static (pool-based) in motor, whereas home liability premium may move with experience.
- **ACV (Actual Cash Value)** - Replacement cost minus depreciation; reflects current market value of the item.
- **RCV (Replacement Cost Value)** - Cost to repair or replace damaged property with new, similar material without deduction for depreciation.
- **FNOL (First Notification of Loss)** - First intimation of a loss to the insurer, via agent, phone, online or even bank.
- **Claim assignment** - Claim is assigned to a claim handler/supervisor (surveyor in India).
- **Investigation** - Examining material facts and whether the loss is genuine or intentional.
- **Estimation** - Putting a number (quantum) on the financial loss.
- **Settlement** - Finalising the payable amount after deductible, depreciation and outstanding amounts.
- **Subrogation** - Insurer's right, after paying, to recover from the third party that caused the loss (or their insurer).
- **Parametric insurance** - Insurer proactively registers and part-pays claims (e.g. 10% or 20%) in a catastrophe or on a defined trigger before investigation.
- **Reserve** - Funds the insurer sets aside based on risk factors.
- **Reinsurance treaties** - Arrangements that help insurers cover large losses.

### Detailed Notes
#### Premium parameters for home insurance
Parameters vary by company and geography, but standard ones are:
- **Location** - State, zip code, distance to wildfire or flood risk zone.
- **Construction** - Type, age of home, material.
- **Replacement cost** of the home.
- **Coverage limit** - Sum insured declared for the house and contents (e.g. 50 lakh vs 1 crore declared furniture gives different premiums).
- **Deductible** - $5,000 vs $10,000 on identical cover: higher deductible lowers premium.
- **Claim history** of homeowner and property, partly to reduce the chance of intentional damage to earn money from insurance.
- **Protective devices** - Automatic sprinklers, alarms lower premium versus homes without them.
- **Credit-based insurance score** - not hard and fast, but low scores or defaults may signal intent to harm property for payout.

#### Deductible and co-pay behaviour
The deductible applies on each claim, not once. Health examples: an OPD benefit paying up to 50% of visits; parents' cover with a 20% co-pay where the first 20% of every claim is borne by the insured, while spouse and children are fully covered.

#### Depreciation, IDV and total loss
Depreciation is typically assumed at 5% to 10% yearly (vehicle bought for 15 lakh is worth less after a year); you can insure higher, such as with zero-depreciation or return-to-invoice riders, but pay extra premium. In a stolen-vehicle case insurers usually pay a lump sum equal to sum insured. In a total loss from an accident, the insurer is not obliged to pay the sum insured: it may repair or offer a similar replacement. Example: a five-year-old car bought for 10 lakh and now worth about 5.5 lakh but insured at 10 lakh. General insurance is subject to indemnity: payment is up to the actual damage, not necessarily the sum insured.

#### Why premiums change at renewal
Motor has own damage and third party. Depreciation matters in own damage; third-party premium stays static regardless of vehicle age or driving history since it is pooled from past accident funds. For homes, liability premium may go up or down with experience. Home premium can also change because of rebuilding/labour cost rises, added items (fence, electrical work, appliances) or area experience (e.g. a sudden wildfire in Canada raising risk next year).

#### ACV vs RCV
RCV is the cost to repair or replace with new similar material without depreciation deduction; ACV is replacement cost minus depreciation (market value). Which one applies depends on policy wording and plan; most modern home policies lean on RCV, while some older policies settle on ACV. The concept applies to autos and other policies too.

#### Claim life cycle
1. **FNOL** - Intimation via agent, phone, online or bank; a claim is opened with bare minimum details.
2. **Claim assignment** - On insurer side; claim handler, supervisor or surveyor visits site, establishes facts, assesses losses; shortfall of documents is also raised here.
3. **Investigation** - Material facts, intentional vs genuine.
4. **Estimation** - Financial quantum only; no emotional factors.
5. **Settlement** - Final amount after deductible, depreciation, outstanding amounts.
6. **Payment** - To the policy owner.
7. **Subrogation** - Recovery from responsible party.
The same steps apply across lines of business. For life: FNOL on death, doctor's certificate for natural death or police report and FIR copy for accident; subrogation generally does not apply.

#### Subrogation example
A truck rams a homeowner's wall. The home insurer pays the homeowner to rebuild, and the homeowner gives the insurer the right to recover from the truck driver or his vehicle insurer under third-party damages. The homeowner does not wait for the other party and claims with their own insurer. Caveat: if the driver was drunk, the truck's insurer may refuse and the driver becomes responsible to reimburse. Physical damage and injury/liability are both covered by the truck's third-party policy.

#### Parametric insurance
In widespread events (floods, fires, earthquakes) insurers may register claims themselves and pay a part (e.g. 10% to 20%) straight away to cover hotel or medical needs, then settle after investigation. Also used in agriculture (drought, flood auto-approval) and, in India, one insurer (Digit) auto-pays flight delay or missed flight claims via API flight tracking. Trainer notes that everyone sells similar policies; service at claim time differentiates insurers.

#### Reserves and reinsurance
Insurers keep reserves based on risk; in catastrophes they rely on reinsurance treaties (the 9/11 losses did not bankrupt insurers).

#### Multiple insurers
You may hold policies with many insurers on one property, but total recovery cannot exceed the actual loss (e.g. insured for 5 crore with 10 insurers; loss of 50 lakh is the maximum payout). It does not matter whether one or many pay.

#### Policy tenure
Own damage is typically one year; third-party is now available up to three years, as is health. Multi-year payment locks premium against increases (health premiums rose 10% to 15% citing medical inflation). Dealerships push three-year policies on new vehicles to earn commission; renewal elsewhere may be cheaper.

#### Modifications and accessories
Accessories and modifications can be covered under auto and home, but must be explicitly declared and legal (street legal); illegal items like an NOS system are not insured.

### Action Items / Q&A / Assumptions
- Q: Is deductible one-time or per claim? A: Per claim.
- Q: How is building depreciation considered in premium? A: About 5% to 10% yearly as a norm, but insurer and customer choose the sum insured; extra riders cost more.
- Q: Does premium fluctuate as property value depreciates? A: Not necessarily; area experience, added items and rebuilding cost also matter.
- Q: When does RCV vs ACV apply? A: Per policy wording and plan; applies beyond homeowner policies.
- Q: In the truck-hits-wall case, whose policy pays? A: Own home insurer pays first, then subrogates against the truck's third-party cover.
- Q: How does FNOL work after mass events? A: Parametric approach, with insurer-initiated claims and part payment.
- Q: How do reserves work in catastrophes? A: Reinsurance treaties, not government help.
- Q: Are claim steps only for home? A: No, standard across lines, with minor extras (e.g. validation).
- Q: Who pays when multiple insurers apply? A: Total capped at actual loss; claim with your own insurer.
- Q: Why is own damage one year and third party up to three years? A: Not necessarily; both are usually one year, with a three-year option for convenience and premium lock.
- Q: Coverage for modifications? A: Yes if declared and legal.
- Assumption: Parameters and depreciation figures are indicative and vary by company and geography. No homework assigned.

### Key Concepts and Definitions

- **Roadside assistance** - Optional coverage under which help is sent to the vehicle and it is moved to a garage.
- **Rental car coverage** - Optional coverage; if covered, the insurer pays for a rental car until repair is completed.
- **Adjuster/claim agent** - Insurer employee (assigned by area) who investigates, gathers pictures, police and medical reports, and prepares the estimate.
- **Deductible** - Described as "like a copay": the amount the policyholder pays first on every claim. Example: $500 deductible, $2,000 estimate - policyholder pays $500, insurer pays $1,500. Zero deductible means higher premium; higher deductible means lower premium (calculated through the rating engine in Guidewire).
- **Underinsured (as explained by trainer)** - Incident caused by a driver who is not licensed to drive (trainer also mentions "below 18"); if this coverage was opted at purchase, the claim is approved.
- **Uninsured (as explained by trainer)** - Incident caused by a driver who is eligible (18+) but not added to the insurance policy or without a licence; if this coverage is in the policy, the claim is approved. The trainer stresses these coverages must be chosen when taking the policy.
- **Total loss ("totaled")** - Vehicle is not repairable, or repair cost exceeds the vehicle's value; the insurer takes the vehicle and pays a lump sum instead of repairing.
- **Settlement agreement** - Agreement reached after negotiation between policyholder/claimant and insurer.
- **Claim closed/settled** - Final status once the cheque number, amount and date are entered in Guidewire.
- **Policy lifecycle (recap)** - Draft, goes to underwriter, approved, terms and conditions read and signed, policy issued.

### Detailed Notes
#### Immediate steps after an incident
- Having an active policy is what matters, regardless of coverage size (10K, 100K, etc.).
- After an incident, call the police. They investigate who is at fault and whether there is bodily injury. If there is injury, an ambulance is called and first aid is given before the injured are moved to hospital.
- If only the vehicle or property is damaged, police prepare an FIR and give a copy to the policyholder.
- Then call the insurance provider (USAA in the example) on its helpline, giving incident location, date, and whether anyone was injured.

#### Registering the claim
- The helpline representative asks for the policy number. If it is not available, the last four digits of the SSN are used; the representative keys it into the Guidewire application and all of the person's policies (auto, home, etc.) are retrieved.
- The representative reviews the policy and coverages, registers the claim and provides a claim number.
- If roadside assistance was opted for, help reaches the vehicle and it is taken to a garage. With rental-car coverage, the policyholder can hire a car from the nearest rental company and the insurer pays until the repair is done.
- Two filing routes: (1) call the agent/helpline with the policy number and details; (2) use the insurer's app (the trainer names State Farm, Geico and others as having apps and is unsure about USAA): open the claim section, fill details, take photos of the damage and area, and submit; no call is needed.

#### Internal review and investigation
- On receiving the claim, the insurer starts an internal review. A claim agent (an insurer employee) is assigned based on who covers the area of the incident (example: Hitech City, Hyderabad used for illustration).
- The agent gathers photos, the police report/FIR and, where someone is hospitalised, medical reports. Witnesses are also investigated.
- Two types of repair garages: the insurer's associated garages (vehicle is towed there) or, if those are far, the nearest garage. The agent examines the vehicle and prepares an estimate to support the claim.
- All evidence (FIR, estimates, police and medical reports) is uploaded to the Guidewire application under the claim, along with the estimate and the deductible amount.

#### Deductible example
- On a policy with a $500 deductible, if the estimate is $2K, the policyholder pays the first $500 and the insurer pays $1,500. This applies to every claim.

#### Fault scenarios
- If another party is at fault, the police assign fault and prepare the FIR accordingly; the incident should not be reported on the not-at-fault driver's driving history, and the at-fault party's insurer handles the claim.
- If the policyholder is at fault, their insurer reimburses the other party.
- Underinsured and uninsured coverages (see definitions) allow the claim to be approved when the at-fault driver is unlicensed or not on any policy.

#### Evaluation and total loss
- Once evidence is uploaded, the underwriter/assessor evaluates the damage and whether it falls within the policy coverage.
- Example from the trainer: vehicle covered for $50,000, repair estimate above $50,000 (e.g., $60K) - the car is totaled and the insurer pays only what the policy covers (the trainer says $40,000 after the $10K deductible in the Q&A discussion). Repair costing more than the vehicle's value makes no sense, so it is marked total.

#### Negotiation, settlement and closure
- After negotiation, a settlement agreement is made between the policyholder/claimant and the insurer.
- In the US everything is paid by cheque: either to the policyholder or directly to the garage; no direct bank deposit.
- The cheque number, amount and date are entered in Guidewire under the claims section, and the claim is closed as settled/closed.

#### Accuracy, fairness, efficiency, and regulation
- Information provided must be accurate; handling must be fair to maintain trust.
- The trainer contrasts the US with India: in the US, businesses are driven by online reviews, customers are treated "like God", and insurers are generally favourable to policyholders because poor reviews affect them heavily (examples given: Walmart returns, rental companies).
- Efficiency means turnaround time, time to settle, and the updates given.
- Legal/regulatory aspects: state insurance departments, compliance requirements, market conduct standards, and customer protections.

### Key Concepts and Definitions
- **Guidewire PolicyCenter** - Policy administration application; left menu has Summary, Activities, Account, Submissions, Renewals. Policy transactions are created from an account.
- **Submission** - A new-business job with a unique submission number; starts in Draft status.

- **Named insured (primary/secondary)** - Primary is the main insured; secondary is optional.
- **Effective / expiration date** - Coverage start date; expiration is calculated by the system.
- **Producer of record** - The agency through which the policy is purchased and which produces it on behalf of the company.
- **Underwriting company** - The insurer from which the policy is bought (e.g., buying through an aggregator: producer = aggregator, underwriting company = the insurer such as Tata AIG).

- **Risk Analysis screen** - Lists underwriter issues; underwriters approve, reject or decline.
- **Static form** - Triggered by default with a coverage (terms/conditions/exclusions); no charge.

- **Bind** - Trainer: policy is in force and coverage starts from that day.
- **Issue** - Completes the policy; status becomes Issued (relevant if documents or items were missing).

### Key Concepts and Definitions
- **Portal / CRM (CSR) application** - Application used by licensed agents; integrated with Guidewire so agent changes reflect in Guidewire and vice versa.
- **Guidewire application** - Used by internal users (the carrier's own employees; "USAA" is the example carrier).
- **PolicyCenter** - The "main heart" of Guidewire where all policy details are stored; integrated with BillingCenter and ClaimCenter.
- **BillingCenter / ClaimCenter** - Billing and claims functions; both pull policy data from PolicyCenter. Their integrations are more limited than PolicyCenter's (e.g., driving history is irrelevant to BillingCenter).
- **Rating engine** - Background application that calculates premiums for new submissions with underwriting input; changes must be deployed before testing.
- **LOB / sub-LOB** - Line of business (e.g., Personal Auto, Homeowners with HO3/HO4/HO5/HO6; commercial lines such as Business Owners, Commercial Auto, Commercial Property, General Liability, Inland Marine, Commercial Umbrella, Workers' Compensation, plus the Commercial Package Policy "CPP"). - **Submission** - A fresh policy request; remains a submission (with a submission number) until the policy is issued and paid.
- **Quote** - A priced calculation of coverages, drivers, vehicles, deductibles and premium; any number of quotes can be created without issuing a policy.
- **Binding / issuance** - Submission is completed, payment made, and the policy becomes active with a policy number.
- **Policy change** - Mid-term change to an issued policy (add driver/vehicle, change coverage limits or deductibles); keeps the same policy number.
- **Renewal** - New term after expiry; processed by a batch job (auto-renewal).
- **Cancellation** - Ending a policy before expiry at the insured's request.
- **Reinstatement** - As described: a temporary cancellation/hold for a period, after which the policy becomes active again with the original expiration date; unused premium is refunded.
- **Rewrite** - Cancelling and re-issuing a policy because of many errors/changes; a policy must be cancelled before it can be rewritten.
- **Audit** - Carrier review of policy data (address, coverage, deductible, premium).
- **VIN (Vehicle Identification Number)** - Unique per vehicle; mandatory to issue a policy.
- **DMV / MVR report** - Driving history, tickets, incidents and vehicle ownership history retrieved through integration.
- **Producer code** - The agent code; identifies which agent (or sub-agent) started the submission; used to credit the agent for policies sold. Pre-populated when an agent logs in.
- **Underwriter referral / risk** - Raised for high-risk items (expensive vehicle, high coverages, multiple speeding tickets); policy cannot issue until the underwriter approves.
- **Underwriting manager** - Monitors subordinates' workloads and reassigns work.
- **First issued date vs effective date** - Issue date is when the policy is created; effective date is when coverage starts and can be current or future.

### Key Concepts and Definitions
- **Personal auto** - Covers vehicles used for personal purposes (cars, bikes).
- **Commercial auto** - Covers business vehicles (trucks, large vehicles); tied to business coverage such as general liability.
- **Purpose of auto insurance** - Transfers the financial risk of owning or driving a vehicle to the insurer in exchange for premium.
- **Quote** - Minimal details (name, address, vehicle details, deductible choice) producing a premium; multiple quotes can be generated and compared before choosing one.
- **Binding** - Agreement between insured and insurer on coverages before policy issuance.
- **Endorsement** - Mid-term change or service request to the policy (also used to notify temporary drivers).
- **Person / entity** - Named insured: a person for personal insurance, a company for commercial.
- **Premium** - Amount paid based on selected coverages.
- **Limit** - Maximum amount the insurer will pay for a covered incident.
- **Exposure** - Risk element used for rating (e.g., driver age, vehicle usage).
- **Deductible** - Amount the insured pays before the insurer pays. Example: a 50K repair with 0 deductible is fully paid by the insurer; with a 20K deductible the insured pays the first 20K.
- **Bodily injury (BI) liability** - Medical expenses of others injured in a crash caused by the insured, up to the limit.
- **Collision** - Damage to the insured's own vehicle from hitting something.
- **UM/UIM** - Uninsured/Underinsured motorist; covers when the at-fault driver has no insurance or insufficient limits (e.g., 200K loss with a 100K limit).
- **Property damage (PD) liability** - Damage to others' property (buildings, cars, fences).
- **Comprehensive** - Non-collision losses: theft, hail, fire, vandalism, animal impact.
- **PIP / medical payments** - Personal injury protection / medical expenses.
- **100/300/100** - Bodily injury per person / per accident / property damage.
- **Territory** - Group of ZIP codes in a state treated as one rating area (traffic and accident frequency).
- **Garaging** - Primary location where the vehicle is kept.
- **MVR (motor vehicle record)** - History of a driver's licence and incidents tracked by the DMV; new insurers pull it.
- **Multi-line discount** - Discount for having more than one policy with the same insurer (e.g., auto plus renters).
- **Telematics** - Insurer-provided GPS device recording speed, distance and locations for a discount.
- **Account / job** - In PolicyCenter, the account is the customer; a job is a transaction (quote, issuance, endorsement).
- **VIN** - Unique vehicle identifier; determines whether a vehicle is personal or commercial.

### Detailed Notes
#### Why auto insurance and personal vs commercial
- Auto insurance falls under property & casualty (casualty). Attendees contrasted personal (cars, bikes for personal use) with commercial (trucks, large vehicles, not for personal use). Commercial auto is mainly used with other business coverage such as general liability.
- Regulation is statewide, not national: each state sets its own coverages, limitations, forms and rules. Pricing comes from the underwriter evaluating risk and calculating price.

#### Policy lifecycle
- Capture of details and coverage choices produces a quote; the submission is made by the agent for the customer or by the customer. Underwriting rules check eligibility and referrals and produce the final quote and premium; binding follows; the policy is issued. Afterwards come service (endorsements/changes), renewal and claims. The lifecycle generalises to other LOBs.

#### Quote explanation (Ravi)
- A policy starts with a quote based on minimal details (first/last name, address, vehicle details) plus deductible. Example from an Indian insurer website: a quote of about 35K for a new car; changing the deductible (e.g., paying 20% of a claim) gives a second quote around 30K. Any number of quotes can be compared for one vehicle.

- In India the trainer's colleague noted own-damage plus mandatory third-party liability. The reply: here a base policy plus additional coverages (riders in India) are combined into premium. More coverages mean higher premium; uncovered incidents are not paid. Mandatory coverages vary by state/carrier; the product design differs business to business.

#### Policy structure and premium factors
- Policy and term (prior insurance, state), drivers (age, experience, family members eligible to drive), vehicle (year, make, model, VIN, garaging), coverages (BI/PD, collision, comprehensive, UM/UIM), terms and modifiers (limits, deductibles, rating factors, discounts).
- Six major factors: drivers (age, history, accidents, violations, experience); vehicle (year/make/model, safety features, usage, value, new/old); location (garaging ZIP, state, territory, local risk such as coastal areas, high traffic); coverage (limits, deductibles, optional coverages); prior history (prior losses and claims); discounts (multiple policies, safety, anti-theft, telematics).

#### Drivers in the US (Ravi)

Up to six drivers can be added to one policy (the trainer's recollection); each added driver can raise the premium.

#### Limits and deductibles

#### Claims history, MVR and discounts
- Insurers see claims history; frequent claims lead to a higher premium or rejection. When switching insurers, applications ask about accidents in the last six months or a year, and the new insurer pulls the DMV/MVR record by licence number. In the US, if an incident is settled without a police report and a claim, it is not recorded in driving history; once a report and claim exist it is tracked.
- Discounts: multi-line (e.g., auto plus renters with State Farm); employee discount (e.g., working for the insurer); telematics device; safety features (airbags, ABS, driver-assistance) and anti-theft (alarm, tracking).

### Key Concepts and Definitions
- **Submission number** - Generated by Guidewire when a submission is initiated for an account and state.
- **Policy Info** - Screen capturing insured, policy type, term, state, effective date and prior insurance; common to all LOBs and used for rating.
- **MVR (Motor Vehicle Record)** - A centralized US record of each driver's licence, accidents and violations; an additional application integrated with PolicyCenter, run automatically from driver details; status shown on the driver screen.
- **Configured (base) coverage** - Base coverages such as liability; optional coverages may be added.
- **Liability coverage (100/300/100)** - First figure: bodily injury per person; second: bodily injury per accident; third: property damage per accident. Part of the base coverage, not an add-on.
- **Collision** - Covers damage to your own car (e.g., hitting a pole), subject to deductible.
- **Medical payments / PIP** - Medical coverage (PIP = personal injury protection).
- **UM/UIM** - Uninsured/underinsured motorist coverage, an additional coverage.
- **Rental and loss of use** - Coverage related to renting a car or being deprived of your own.
- **Risk analysis / underwriter issue** - Screen where the system raises underwriting issues or blockers needing additional information.
- **Rating engine inputs (personal auto)** - Driver risk, vehicle risk, location and coverage.
- **Policy change** - Modification such as adding/removing a driver or coverage.
- **Rewrite** - Recreating the entire policy.
- **Commercial auto** - Insures businesses/organizations with employee drivers; vehicles may be company-owned, hired or leased (cars, vans, pickup trucks, fleets).
- **Scheduled vehicle** - A specifically identified vehicle (e.g., two or three) under the business policy.
- **Broader covered auto options** - Coverage for many vehicles under the business.
- **HNOA (hired and non-owned auto)** - Liability exposure for vehicles used for business but not owned by it (e.g., an employee's car, Uber-type drivers' vehicles).
- **Radius of operation** - Distance range a commercial vehicle operates; asked in commercial underwriting.
- **Commercial general liability (CGL)** - Separate business insurance that can cover professional/employee-related damages.
- **Modifiers (commercial auto)** - Two kinds: liability rates and physical damage rates, applied as credit or debit.
